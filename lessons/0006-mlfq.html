Lesson 6 · CPU scheduling

MLFQ: a scheduler that learns

SJF and STCF were great, but they needed to know every job's length in advance. MLFQ never asks. It watches how each job behaves and adjusts its priority, so short and interactive jobs float to the top and long CPU hogs sink to the bottom.

The one-sentence version The Multi-Level Feedback Queue keeps several queues of different priority, always runs the highest non-empty queue, and moves jobs between queues based on how much CPU they have used. It is "a scheduler that learns from the past to predict the future".Bilal slides, Ch 8 p.4

STCF. X arrives at 0 (burst 5), Y arrives at 1 (burst 2). Give the timeline and the average turnaround. (Lesson 5)

At t = 1, Y (2) < X's 4 left → preempt.
X 0–1 | Y 1–3 | X 3–7.
Turnaround: X 7 − 0 = 7, Y 3 − 1 = 2 → 9/2 = 4.5. (Both have response 0.)

Round Robin: what does RR become if the time slice is longer than every job? And which metric does RR optimise, which does it hurt? (Lesson 5)

No job is ever cut short, so RR runs jobs in arrival order: it becomes FIFO. RR is good for response time (and fairness) but bad for turnaround.

Any scheduler that takes the CPU away mid-run (like MLFQ demoting a job) depends on one piece of hardware. Which, and what would happen without it? (Lesson 4)

The timer interrupt. Without it the OS only gets control when a process makes a system call, yields or faults (cooperative). A CPU-bound job could run forever and the OS could never demote or switch it.

The two goals, and why they clash

Dr. Bilal states the objective in two lines:Bilal slides, Ch 8 p.4–5

GoalIdeal policyThe catch
1. Optimize turnaround timeRun shorter jobs first (like SJF)The OS usually does not know job lengths in advance, so SJF can't be applied directly
2. Minimize response time for interactive jobsRound RobinRR improves response time but hurts turnaround time

Key question: how do you balance these two conflicting goals without knowing job lengths upfront? Answer: learn them. A job's past behaviour is a decent guess about its future.

Analogy (from the slides) A busy café. Some customers want a quick coffee, others a full meal, and you can't ask how long each will take. So you serve everyone a little at first. Whoever keeps ordering more gets moved to the "long table" and served after the quick customers.

What two goals does MLFQ try to meet at the same time, and why can't it just use SJF for the first one?

(1) Optimise turnaround (run short jobs first, like SJF). (2) Minimise response time for interactive jobs (like RR). It can't use SJF because the OS doesn't know job lengths in advance, so MLFQ learns them from past behaviour.

Which is TRUE about what MLFQ is trying to do?

  • Optimise turnaround like SJF and minimise response time like RR, without knowing job lengths in advance. Both goals at once, learning each job's nature from its past behaviour.
  • Optimise turnaround like RR and minimise response time like SJF, without knowing job lengths in advance. Swapped: SJF is the turnaround policy and RR the response-time policy.
  • Optimise turnaround like SJF and minimise response time like RR, using each job's length given in advance. The whole point: the OS doesn't know job lengths, so MLFQ learns them.
  • Optimise turnaround like SJF and minimise response time like RR, by giving each job a fixed priority. MLFQ varies a job's priority based on its observed behaviour (OSTEP); a fixed priority couldn't learn anything.

Why do MLFQ's two goals clash?

  • SJF can't be used because job lengths are unknown, and RR improves response time but hurts turnaround. Exactly Dr. Bilal's two "challenge" lines.
  • SJF can't be used because job lengths are unknown, and RR improves turnaround but hurts response time. Reversed: RR improves response and hurts turnaround.
  • SJF can't be used because it is preemptive, and RR improves response time but hurts turnaround. SJF is non-preemptive. The obstacle is that job lengths are unknown.
  • SJF can't be used because job lengths are unknown, and RR improves both response time and turnaround. If RR improved both, there would be no clash. It hurts turnaround by stretching every job out.

The structure: a ladder of queues

MLFQ has several distinct queues, and each queue has a different priority level. A ready job sits on exactly one queue at a time. Dr. Bilal numbers them so the highest number is the highest priority (with 3 queues: Q2 top, Q0 bottom).Bilal slides, Ch 8 p.9–10

In MLFQ, how many queues can a ready job be on at once, and which queue's jobs get the CPU?

A ready job is on exactly one queue. The scheduler always runs a job from the highest-priority non-empty queue.

A 3-queue MLFQ: which queue is the top (highest priority) in Dr. Bilal's numbering, and which in Dr. Zafar's?

Dr. Bilal: Q2 is the top (highest number = highest priority), Q0 the bottom. Dr. Zafar: Queue 1 is the top. Use whatever the question uses.

Which is TRUE about MLFQ's queues?

  • A ready job is on exactly one queue, and the scheduler runs a job from the highest-priority non-empty queue. Each queue is one priority level; Rule 1 says the higher queue always wins.
  • A ready job is on every queue at or below its priority, and the scheduler runs a job from the highest-priority non-empty queue. A ready job is on exactly one queue at a time.
  • A ready job is on exactly one queue, and the scheduler gives every non-empty queue one slice in turn. Queues don't take turns. A lower queue runs only when every higher queue is empty.
  • A ready job is on exactly one queue, and the scheduler runs whichever job has waited longest, on any queue. Waiting time doesn't decide it: priority does (highest non-empty queue first).

A and B are on the top queue, C on the middle queue, D on the bottom queue. All four stay ready, nobody moves between queues, and there is no boost. Which is TRUE?

  • A and B take turns in RR; C runs only when the top queue is empty; D runs only when both higher queues are empty. Rule 2 within a level, Rule 1 between levels.
  • A runs to completion before B; C runs only when the top queue is empty; D runs only when both higher queues are empty. Jobs on the same queue share in RR (Rule 2), not one after another.
  • A and B take turns in RR; C and D take turns in RR whenever the top queue is empty. C and D have different priorities, so C runs first; D waits until C's queue is empty too.
  • A and B take turns in RR; C runs only when the top queue is empty; D gets a slice every few rounds so it doesn't starve. Nothing gives D a turn while higher jobs stay ready. That is starvation. The fix is the priority boost (Rule 5), coming later in this lesson; the question says there is no boost.

The five rules (learn these word for word)

Here is the final rule set exactly as on Dr. Bilal's recap slide.Bilal slides, Ch 8 p.24OSTEP §8.6

  • Rule 1 If Priority(A) > Priority(B), A runs (B doesn't).
  • Rule 2 If Priority(A) = Priority(B), A & B run in RR.
  • Rule 3 When a job enters the system, it is placed at the highest priority.
  • Rule 4 Once a job uses up its time allotment at a given level (regardless of how many times it has given up the CPU), its priority is reduced (i.e., it moves down the queue).
  • Rule 5 After some time period S, move all the jobs in the system to the topmost queue.
This is how it's asked "What are the 5 rules of MLFQ?" is a 5-mark question in Dr. Bilal's quizzes, and he expects these sentences. A memory hook: 1–2 pick (who runs), 3 start (where new jobs go), 4 sink (how jobs move down), 5 boost (how they get back up).

The rules didn't arrive all at once. The slides build them in two attempts, and each fix exists because of a specific problem. Knowing that story is what lets you answer the "why" questions.

Write down the 5 rules of MLFQ.

MLFQ: which rule numbers decide (a) who runs, (b) where a new job starts, (c) how a job moves down, (d) how jobs get back up?

(a) Rules 1–2 (pick). (b) Rule 3 (start at the top). (c) Rule 4 (sink after the allotment). (d) Rule 5 (boost every S).

Which is TRUE about MLFQ Rule 1?

  • If Priority(A) > Priority(B), A runs and B doesn't. A lower queue gets the CPU only when every higher queue is empty.
  • If Priority(A) > Priority(B), A and B run in RR. RR is Rule 2, for equal priority. Higher priority simply wins.
  • If Priority(A) > Priority(B), B runs whenever A's time slice ends. When A's slice ends, A (or another job at its level) runs again. B waits until no higher-priority job is ready.
  • If Priority(A) > Priority(B), A runs, unless B has less work left. MLFQ doesn't know how much work is left. It picks by priority only.

Which is TRUE about MLFQ Rule 2?

  • If Priority(A) = Priority(B), A & B run in RR. Jobs on the same queue take turns, one time slice each.
  • If Priority(A) = Priority(B), they run FIFO, each to completion. Same-queue jobs share in round robin, one slice each.
  • If Priority(A) = Priority(B), the one with less time left runs first. That is STCF, which needs run-times MLFQ doesn't know. Equal priority → RR.

Which is TRUE about where MLFQ puts a new job?

  • A new job is placed at the highest priority (the top queue). Rule 3: every job gets the benefit of the doubt, as if it might be short.
  • A new job is placed at the lowest priority and moves up if it proves interactive. Rule 3 puts it at the top. MLFQ has no rule that promotes a job level by level.
  • A new job is placed in the queue that matches its expected run-time. MLFQ never knows run-times; every new job starts at the top.
  • A new job is placed in the middle queue until its behaviour is known. Rule 3: highest priority, the top queue.

Which is TRUE about MLFQ's final Rule 4?

  • Once a job's total time at a level reaches its allotment, it moves down one queue. Its time at the level is added up across all its stints; when the total reaches the allotment, its priority is reduced by one level.
  • Once a job uses up its time allotment at a level, it drops straight to the bottom queue. It moves down one queue at a time (Q2 → Q1 → Q0), as in the slides' single long-running job example.
  • A job moves down only if it uses a whole time slice in one go without giving up the CPU. That was old Rule 4a, which could be gamed. Rule 4 adds up all its time at the level.
  • A job's used allotment resets to zero each time it gives up the CPU for I/O. That reset is exactly what the rewrite removed: time counts in total, "regardless of how many times it has given up the CPU".

Which is TRUE about MLFQ Rule 5?

  • After some time period S, all jobs move to the topmost queue and can sink again later. The boost is a fresh start, not a permanent pass: Rule 4 still applies afterwards.
  • After some time period S, all jobs move to the topmost queue and stay there until they finish. The classic diagram mistake. After the boost a CPU-bound job uses one slice per level and walks down again.
  • After some time period S, only the jobs in the bottom queue move up one level. Rule 5 moves all jobs, and all the way to the topmost queue.
  • After some time period S, all jobs move down one queue to make room for new jobs. Reversed: the boost moves everyone up to the top.

Attempt 1: Rules 3, 4a and 4b

The first version of "how priority changes" was:Bilal slides, Ch 8 p.11

  • Rule 3 When a job enters the system, it is placed at the highest priority.
  • Rule 4a If a job uses up an entire time slice while running, its priority is reduced (i.e., it moves down the queue).
  • Rule 4b If a job gives up the CPU before the time slice is up, it stays at the same priority level.

The logic: every new job gets the benefit of the doubt and starts at the top, as if it might be short. If it really is short, it finishes quickly up there, just like SJF. If it keeps burning whole slices, it is probably long and CPU-bound, so it sinks. In this manner, MLFQ approximates SJF, without ever being told a job length.

Example: along came a short job

Three levels with slices Q2 = 2, Q1 = 4, Q0 = run to completion. Job A is long (14 units) and starts at 0. Short job B (3 units) arrives at t = 5.

  1. A starts in Q2 (Rule 3), uses its full slice of 2, and drops to Q1 (Rule 4).
  2. B arrives at t = 5 and enters Q2. Q2 is higher than Q1, so B takes over immediately (Rule 1). A has already used 3 of its 4 units in Q1, and it keeps that count.
  3. B uses its 2 units in Q2 and drops to Q1, behind A. A uses its last unit of Q1 allotment (7–8) and drops to Q0.
  4. B finishes in Q1 at t = 9 (turnaround 4). A then runs alone in Q0.

Nobody told the scheduler B was short. It behaved like SJF anyway.

State Rules 4a and 4b from the first attempt at MLFQ.

4a: If a job uses up an entire time slice while running, its priority is reduced. 4b: If a job gives up the CPU before the time slice is up, it stays at the same priority level.

How does MLFQ approximate SJF? Which is TRUE?

  • Every job starts at the top, so a short job finishes before it sinks. Long jobs keep using whole slices and sink; short ones are done while still at high priority, so they effectively run first, like SJF.
  • Every job states its run-time on entry, and MLFQ runs the shortest first. MLFQ is never told run-times; it learns them from how the job behaves.
  • Every job starts at the bottom, so long jobs never delay short ones. Rule 3 puts new jobs at the top.
  • Every queue is sorted by remaining time, so the shortest job runs first. Inside a queue, jobs run in RR (Rule 2). MLFQ doesn't know remaining times.

Three problems with Attempt 1

Dr. Bilal lists three issues with the basic MLFQ:Bilal slides, Ch 8 p.16

ProblemWhat goes wrongFix
StarvationToo many interactive jobs keep the top queues busy. Long-running jobs sit at the bottom and never get sufficient CPU time.Rule 5: priority boost
Gaming the schedulerA process runs for 99% of its slice, then does a tiny I/O just before the slice ends. Under Rule 4b it never moves down, so it keeps high priority and takes more CPU than is fair.Rule 4 rewritten: better accounting
Changing behaviourA CPU-bound process becomes interactive later (crunches data, then waits for the user). It already sank, and nothing ever moves it back up.Rule 5: priority boost

Fix 1: the priority boost (Rule 5)

Every S time units, move every job to the topmost queue. One rule, two problems solved:Bilal slides, Ch 8 p.18–19OSTEP §8.3

  • No starvation: a job stuck in Q0 is guaranteed to reach the top within S and will share the CPU with the interactive jobs there in RR.
  • Changed behaviour is noticed: a job that has turned interactive gets a fresh start at the top and can stay there by giving up the CPU early.

Choosing S is tricky: too large and long jobs still starve for a while; too small and interactive jobs don't get their proper share. OSTEP calls values like this "voodoo constants".

The classic diagram mistake A boost puts the job back at the top, but it does not give it a permanent pass. A CPU-bound job that is boosted to Q2 must run one Q2 slice, drop to Q1, run one Q1 slice, then drop to Q0 again. A diagram that shows the job staying in Q2 after the boost is wrong.

Wrong: after the boost at t = 20, A stays in Q2 until it finishes.

Right: after the boost, A walks down the ladder again: 2 units in Q2, 4 in Q1, then Q0.

State the rule that is broken and what the correct picture would show.

Fix 2: better accounting (Rule 4 replaces 4a and 4b)

The gaming trick works because Rule 4b wipes the slate clean every time a job gives up the CPU. The fix is to keep a running total instead:Bilal slides, Ch 8 p.21

Rule 4 (rewrite of Rules 4a & 4b) Once a job exhausts its total time allocation at a given priority level, no matter how many times it voluntarily relinquishes the CPU, its priority is lowered (i.e., it is moved down to a lower queue).

Now the sneaky job can split its time into as many small pieces as it likes. Once the pieces add up to the allotment, it moves down.

A job repeatedly runs 9 ms of its 10 ms slice and then does a 1 ms I/O. What happens to its priority under old Rules 4a/4b, and under the new Rule 4?

Old rules: it never uses a whole slice, so by 4b it stays at the top forever and grabs most of the CPU. New Rule 4: its 9 ms pieces add up; once the total reaches the 10 ms allotment it is moved down.

MLFQ slices 3 / 6 / FCFS. A long CPU-bound job sits in the bottom queue when the priority boost fires. What does a correct diagram show next? And what goes wrong if S is too large or too small?

It moves to the top, runs one top slice (3), drops and runs one middle slice (6), then drops back to the bottom. It does not stay at the top. S too large → long jobs still starve for a while; S too small → interactive jobs lose their share.

Which is TRUE about starvation in first-attempt MLFQ?

  • Too many interactive jobs hog the top queues, so long jobs starve; the Rule 5 boost fixes it. Every S, the boost lifts the starving long jobs to the top, where they share the CPU in RR.
  • Too many interactive jobs hog the top queues, so long jobs starve; the rewritten Rule 4 fixes it. Rule 4 fixes gaming. Starvation is fixed by the Rule 5 boost.
  • Long jobs hog the top queues, so interactive jobs starve; the Rule 5 boost fixes it. Reversed. Long jobs sink to the bottom; it is the long-running jobs that starve.
  • Too many interactive jobs hog the top queues, so long jobs starve; Rule 3 fixes it. Rule 3 only places new jobs at the top. The fix is the Rule 5 boost.

Which is TRUE about gaming the scheduler?

  • A job does I/O just before its slice ends, so old Rule 4b keeps it on top; the rewritten Rule 4 fixes it. Rule 4 counts the job's total time at the level, so its small pieces add up and it moves down.
  • A job does I/O just before its slice ends, so old Rule 4b keeps it on top; the Rule 5 boost fixes it. The boost lifts everyone, the gamer included. The fix is Rule 4's better accounting.
  • A job does I/O just before its slice ends, so old Rule 4a demotes it unfairly; Rule 4 fixes it. The gamer escapes demotion: it never uses a whole slice, so 4a never fires and 4b keeps it on top.
  • A job does I/O just before its slice ends, so old Rule 4b keeps it on top; Rule 4 fixes it by resetting the allotment after each I/O. Resetting was the bug. Rule 4 accumulates the time instead.

A CPU-bound job sank to the bottom queue, then turned interactive. Which is TRUE?

  • The Rule 5 boost gives it a fresh start at the top, where its new behaviour lets it stay high. This is the "changing behaviour" problem, and the priority boost is its fix.
  • Rule 4b moves it up a level each time it gives up the CPU early. 4b only keeps a job at its same level. Nothing but the boost (Rule 5) moves jobs up.
  • Rule 3 moves it back to the top, because it now behaves like a new job. Rule 3 applies only when a job enters the system. The fix is the Rule 5 boost.
  • It stays at the bottom for good, because MLFQ has no way to notice the change. That was the problem with the first attempt. Rule 5 (the boost) fixes it.

The allotment at the top level is 10 ms. A job runs 4 ms, does a quick I/O, and repeats. Under the rewritten Rule 4, which is TRUE?

  • It moves down during its 3rd run, when its total at the level reaches 10 ms. 4 + 4 = 8 ms after two runs; 2 ms into the third run the total hits 10 ms, so it is demoted.
  • It moves down after its 2nd run, once its total reaches 8 ms. 4 + 4 = 8 < 10, still under the allotment. It drops during the 3rd run, when the total reaches 10.
  • It never moves down, because it always gives up the CPU before its slice ends. That was true under old Rule 4b. Rule 4 adds the pieces up: 4 + 4 + 2 = 10 → demoted in the 3rd run.

Tuning: different slices for different queues

The rules don't say how many queues to use, how long each slice is, or what S should be. A common choice:Bilal slides, Ch 8 p.22–23

  • High-priority queues get short slices. They hold interactive jobs, which only need a moment, so switching quickly keeps response time low.
  • Low-priority queues get long slices. They hold CPU-bound jobs, so fewer context switches means less overhead. Example: 10 ms top, 20 ms middle, 40 ms bottom.
  • Solaris (Time-Sharing class): 60 queues, slices from 20 ms at the top up to a few hundred ms at the bottom, priorities boosted about every 1 second.

Why do MLFQ implementations usually give high-priority queues short time slices and low-priority queues long ones?

Top queues hold interactive jobs that need only a brief turn, so short slices keep response time low. Bottom queues hold CPU-bound jobs, so long slices mean fewer context switches (less overhead). Example: 10 / 20 / 40 ms.

Solaris's Time-Sharing MLFQ: roughly how many queues, what slice range, and how often is the boost?

60 queues, slices from 20 ms (top) up to a few hundred ms (bottom), priorities boosted about every 1 second.

Which is TRUE about time slices in a tuned MLFQ?

  • High-priority queues get short slices; low-priority queues get long slices. Top queues hold interactive jobs that need only a brief turn (good response); bottom queues hold CPU-bound jobs, where long slices mean fewer switches. E.g. 10 / 20 / 40 ms.
  • High-priority queues get long slices; low-priority queues get short slices. Swapped. Short slices at the top, long slices at the bottom.
  • Every queue gets the same slice, because Rule 2 runs each queue in RR. Rule 2 says nothing about slice length. Tuned MLFQs vary it, e.g. 10 / 20 / 40 ms, and Solaris goes from 20 ms to a few hundred ms.
  • High-priority queues get short slices so that long CPU-bound jobs finish sooner. Right layout, wrong reason. Short top slices keep interactive jobs responsive; CPU-bound jobs live in the low queues with long slices.

In an MLFQ trace, a job's burst ends exactly at the end of its slice. Does it move down? And in what order do jobs run inside one queue?

No: it has finished, so it simply leaves. Inside one queue, jobs run in RR in the order they arrived in that queue.

MLFQ: top slice 2, middle slice 4, bottom FCFS, no boost. A (burst 6), B (burst 3), C (burst 2) all arrive at t = 0 in that order. Which is TRUE?

  • The average turnaround time is 9. Top: A 0–2, B 2–4, C 4–6 (done). Middle: A 6–10 (done), B 10–11 (done). Turnaround (10 + 11 + 6)/3 = 27/3 = 9.
  • The average turnaround time is 8. That runs B before A in the middle queue (B 6–7, A 7–11 → 24/3). Inside a queue, jobs run in the order they arrived there: A dropped first. Answer 9.
  • The average turnaround time is 11. 11 is when the last job (B) finishes. Average: (10 + 11 + 6)/3 = 9.
  • The average turnaround time is 2. That is the average response time, (0 + 2 + 4)/3. Turnaround uses completion: 9.

Same trace (slices 2 / 4 / FCFS; A 6, B 3, C 2, all at t = 0). Which is TRUE?

  • C's burst ends at t = 6, exactly when its top slice does, so it finishes and never moves down. A job that finishes at the end of its slice simply leaves the system.
  • C uses its whole top slice from 4 to 6, so it drops to the middle queue and finishes there. C needed only 2, so it is finished at 6. A finished job doesn't move down.
  • B runs before A in the middle queue, because B has less work left than A. MLFQ doesn't know remaining work. Inside a queue it is RR in arrival order there: A (dropped at 2) before B (dropped at 4).
  • A uses its full middle slice from 6 to 10, so it drops to the bottom queue before finishing. A had exactly 6 − 2 = 4 left, so it finishes at 10. The bottom queue is never used here.

Look at what is not here: when a job goes to the back of its queue on line 178, allot_left is not reset. It keeps counting down across turns, and is refilled only on demotion (line 170) or boost. A bigger middle allotment keeps the long job in the middle queue for longer. That is the "different slices for different queues" idea from Tuning.

Your MLFQ1 trace starts 1, 2, 3, 1, 2, 3, 1, 2, 3 in the top queue. What would the first 9 ticks be if you changed RR_TIME_SLICE to 3 (everything else the same)?

1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 2, 3, 3, 3, then all three drop to the middle queue. Each job now uses its full 3-tick allotment in one turn. (Checked by running a copy.)

In MLFQ2 (allotments 3 / 6 / 12), Job 3 never reaches the bottom queue after the second boost at t = 30. Use the numbers to explain why.

Job 3 needs 30 ticks in total and has done 21 by t = 30, so 9 are left. After the boost it runs 3 in the top queue (ticks 31–33), drops, then needs only 6 more, which is exactly the middle allotment (34–39). It finishes at the end of that allotment, so it never moves down. A job that finishes exactly when its allotment runs out doesn't drop a level.

How would you make mlfq.c count time per turn instead of total time at a level (the accounting hole behind gaming the scheduler)? Which branch, and what line?

In the Rule 2 branch (lines 177–181), refill the allotment when the job goes back in its queue: add jobs[current_job_index].allot_left = quanta[jobs[current_job_index].current_queue]; before the enqueue. Now the count restarts every turn, so with a 1-tick slice and an allotment of 3 no job would ever move down. That per-turn counting is the old Rules 4a/4b idea, the hole a sneaky job uses to game the scheduler.

Which line in run_mlfq is Rule 3 (a new job starts at the highest priority)?

  • jobs[i].current_queue = 0; In mlfq.c queue 0 is the top (the first one the pick loop scans), so every job starts there.
  • jobs[i].current_queue = Q_LEVELS - 1; That is Dr. Bilal's numbering (highest number on top). In this code, queue Q_LEVELS − 1 = 2 is the bottom.
  • jobs[i].current_queue = 1; The printout calls the top column "Q1", but the array index of the top queue is 0.
  • jobs[i].current_queue = Q_LEVELS; Index 3 is past the end of a 3-level array (valid indexes are 0–2). The top is 0.

MLFQ2 (quanta2 = {3, 6, 12}). A job has just used up its allotment in the middle queue. Which is TRUE right after lines 166–171?

  • It is in queue 2 with allot_left = 12. next_q = 1 + 1 = 2, and line 170 sets allot_left = quanta[2] = 12.
  • It is in queue 2 with allot_left = 6. 6 is the allotment of the level it just left. Line 170 uses quanta[next_q] = 12.
  • It is in queue 0 with allot_left = 3. That is what a boost does (Rule 5). Running out of allotment moves it down, to queue 2.
  • It is in queue 1 with allot_left = 12. It moves down a level: next_q = current_queue + 1 = 2.

After a 1-tick turn, Job 3 is sent to the back of its queue by the Rule 2 branch (line 178). Which is TRUE about its allot_left?

  • It stays where it is and keeps counting down on later turns. Only demotion (line 170) and the boost refill it. Counting total time at a level is the rewritten Rule 4.
  • It is refilled to quanta of its current queue. That is per-turn counting (the old 4a/4b idea), which lets a job game the scheduler. The Rule 2 branch never touches allot_left.
  • It is refilled to quanta[0]. Apart from the start (line 71), only the boost (lines 89 and 97) sets quanta[0]. The Rule 2 branch doesn't change it.
  • It is set to 0, so the job drops a level on its next turn. Nothing sets it to 0. It drops only once its own count reaches 0 (line 165).

Practice

Answer from memory first, then check. Struggling a little is what makes it stick.

1. MLFQ with three queues: top slice 2, middle slice 4, bottom FCFS. Jobs P1 (burst 3), P2 (burst 9) and P3 (burst 5) all arrive at t = 0 in that order. No boost. At what time does P2 finish?

Top: P1 0–2, P2 2–4, P3 4–6 (all drop). Middle: P1 6–7 (finishes, it only needed 1 more), P2 7–11 (5 left, drops), P3 11–14 (finishes, needed 3). Bottom: P2 14–17. P2 finishes at 17.

2. Same three jobs as Q1. What is the average turnaround time?

P1 finishes at 7, P2 at 17, P3 at 14, all arrived at 0. (7 + 17 + 14) / 3 = 38 / 3 ≈ 12.67.

3. A program runs for 99% of its time slice, issues a tiny I/O, and repeats. Which rule stops it from staying at top priority?

This is gaming the scheduler. The rewritten Rule 4 counts the job's total time at a level, however many times it gives up the CPU. Once that total reaches the allotment, the job moves down. (Rule 5 fixes starvation and changing behaviour, not gaming.)

4. Slices are 2 / 4 / FCFS and the boost period is S = 20. A is long and B has burst 5. In this chart, what is wrong?

Everything up to t = 22 is right: B (burst 5) finishes in Q1 at 11, and at t = 20 the boost lifts A to Q2, where it correctly uses one slice of 2. But then A jumps straight to Q0. It must first use its Q1 allotment of 4 (Q1 22–26) and only then drop to Q0 (26–32).

5. A text editor runs for about 1 ms after each keystroke, then waits for the next key. Under MLFQ, where does it spend most of its life?

It gives up the CPU long before its slice is used, so it stays at its level (Rule 4b in the first attempt; with Rule 4 its 1 ms pieces add up very slowly, and Rule 5 lifts it back anyway). Interactive jobs stay near the top, which is exactly how MLFQ gets good response time.

6. Why do MLFQ implementations usually give high-priority queues shorter time slices?

The top queues hold interactive jobs that only need a moment of CPU, so short slices let them take turns quickly (good response time). The bottom queues hold CPU-bound jobs, where longer slices mean fewer context switches and less overhead.
7. Write down the 5 rules of MLFQ (exact wording).
Rule 1: If Priority(A) > Priority(B), A runs (B doesn't).
Rule 2: If Priority(A) = Priority(B), A & B run in RR.
Rule 3: When a job enters the system, it is placed at the highest priority.
Rule 4: Once a job uses up its time allotment at a given level (regardless of how many times it has given up the CPU), its priority is reduced (i.e., it moves down the queue).
Rule 5: After some time period S, move all the jobs in the system to the topmost queue.
8. Name the three problems with the basic MLFQ (Rules 1–3, 4a, 4b) and which rule fixes each.
Starvation: too many interactive jobs mean long jobs never run. Fixed by Rule 5 (priority boost). Gaming the scheduler: doing I/O just before the slice ends keeps a job at high priority. Fixed by the rewritten Rule 4 (better accounting of total time at a level). Changing behaviour: a CPU-bound job that turns interactive is stuck at the bottom. Fixed by Rule 5 (it gets a fresh start at the top).
