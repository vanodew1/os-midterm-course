Lesson 7 · CPU scheduling

Lottery and stride: sharing by tickets

MLFQ tries to guess which jobs are short. This lesson asks a different question. What fraction of the CPU should each job get? Then the scheduler makes sure each job gets that fraction.

The one-sentence version A proportional-share (fair-share) scheduler guarantees that each job obtains a certain percentage of CPU time. It is not optimized for turnaround or response time.Bilal slides, Ch 9A p.3 There are two ways to do it. Lottery draws random tickets, so it hits the percentages on average. Stride keeps a counter for each job, so it hits them exactly.

Under MLFQ, which rule stops a job gaming the scheduler, and how? (Lesson 6)

The rewritten Rule 4: once a job uses up its time allotment at a level, it moves down, no matter how many times it gave up the CPU. Its small pieces add up, so doing I/O just before the slice ends no longer helps.

Which MLFQ rule fixes both starvation and changing behaviour, and what does it do? (Lesson 6)

Rule 5, the priority boost: after some time period S, move all jobs to the topmost queue. Starving long jobs get CPU again, and a job that turned interactive gets a fresh start at the top.

Round Robin, time slice 3. A (burst 4) and B (burst 3) arrive at t = 0, A first. Average turnaround and response? (Lesson 5)

A 0–3 | B 3–6 (done) | A 6–7 (done).
Turnaround: (7 + 6)/2 = 6.5. Response: (0 + 3)/2 = 1.5.

Tickets represent a share

The problem: we want to say "A gets 75% of the CPU, B gets 25%". How do we write that down in a way the scheduler can use?

The book's answer is a raffle. A ticket is a token that stands for a share of the CPU. The more tickets a job holds, the bigger its share.Bilal slides, Ch 9A p.4

The rule: a job's share = its tickets ÷ all the tickets.

Example: A holds 75 tickets and B holds 25. Total = 100. So A should get 75/100 = 75% of the CPU and B 25%.

In short: share = your tickets ÷ total tickets. Proportional share is about percentages of CPU, not turnaround or response time.

What does a proportional-share (fair-share) scheduler guarantee, and what is it not optimised for?

It guarantees that each job gets a certain percentage of CPU time (its share of the tickets). It is not optimised for turnaround or response time.

A holds 15 tickets, B 45 and C 90. What share of the CPU should each get?

Total = 150. A = 15/150 = 10%, B = 45/150 = 30%, C = 90/150 = 60%.

Which is TRUE about a proportional-share (fair-share) scheduler?

  • It tries to guarantee each job a certain percentage of CPU time. A job's share is its tickets divided by the total (lottery gets there on average, stride exactly). It is not optimised for turnaround or response time.
  • It tries to guarantee the lowest possible average turnaround time. Proportional share is explicitly not optimised for turnaround (or response). It targets percentages.
  • It tries to guarantee each job the same percentage, whatever its tickets. The share is proportional to tickets: 75 of 100 tickets → 75%.
  • It tries to guarantee each job a bounded response time. It is not optimised for response either. It guarantees a percentage of CPU time.

Lottery scheduling

The problem: we have tickets. How do we turn them into "who runs next?" so that A really ends up with about 75%?

Hold a lottery every time slice. The scheduler picks a winning ticket at random. The process holding that ticket runs for one slice. A job with 75 of 100 tickets wins about 75% of the draws.Bilal slides, Ch 9A p.5OSTEP §9.1

Step 2 · Draw, then walk the list

The code doesn't store the ranges. It keeps the jobs in a list and works the ranges out as it goes:

  1. Draw a random winning number.
  2. Walk the list. Add each job's tickets to a running total called counter.
  3. Stop at the first job where the counter goes past the winner (counter > winner). That job runs.

Bilal slides, Ch 9A p.9OSTEP Fig 9.1

winner  = random(0, totaltickets - 1);
counter = 0;
for (job = head; job; job = job->next) {
    counter += job->tickets;
    if (counter > winner) break;     // found the winner
}
run(job);

Example: list order D (30) → K (20) → W (50), total 100, winning ticket 62.

  1. D: counter = 30. Is 30 > 62? No, keep going.
  2. K: counter = 30 + 20 = 50. Is 50 > 62? No, keep going.
  3. W: counter = 50 + 50 = 100. Is 100 > 62? Yes. W runs.

The list order changes who owns which numbers. It does not change anyone's share. Putting the biggest ticket holder first just makes the walk shorter on average.

Lottery. The list is P (20 tickets) → Q (35) → R (45), the winning ticket is 40, and the walk stops when counter > winner. Which is TRUE?

  • Q runs: the counter goes 20, then 55, and 55 > 40. P: is 20 > 40? No. Q: is 55 > 40? Yes, stop. Q runs.
  • P runs: its counter of 20 is the first value below 40. Reversed test. The walk stops when the counter goes past the winner; 20 > 40 is false, so it keeps going.
  • R runs: it holds the most tickets, so the walk checks it first. The walk follows the list order (P, Q, R), not ticket size. Counter 20, then 55 > 40 → Q.
  • R runs: the counter goes 20, 55, then 100, which ends the walk. The walk stops at the first job whose counter passes the winner: 55 > 40, so Q.

Fair only in the long run

The problem: lottery uses luck. How close does it really get to the ticket shares, and when does it miss?

Lottery is probabilistic: it depends on chance. Think of tossing a coin. Ten tosses might give 7 heads. A thousand tosses give close to 500.

Lottery works the same way. Nothing stops B from winning three times in a row. On Dr. Bilal's slide, A holds 75 of 100 tickets, yet B wins 4 of the 15 draws shown (27%, not 25%). "The longer these two jobs compete, the more likely they are to achieve the desired percentages."Bilal slides, Ch 9A p.6

Measuring it: the unfairness metric U

How do we put a number on "how unfair"? Run two jobs with the same runtime and the same tickets. A perfectly fair scheduler would finish them at almost the same moment. So compare their finish times.

Unfairness metric U = (time the first job completes) / (time the second job completes). U = 1 is perfectly fair, and a U far below 1 is unfair.Bilal slides, Ch 9A p.10

Example: both jobs need 20 time units. One finishes at 28 and the other at 40. So U = 28 / 40 = 0.7. OSTEP's study shows that U is poor when jobs are short. It only gets close to 1 when jobs run for a long time (many draws, like many coin tosses).OSTEP Fig 9.2

Final count: P 3, Q 3, R 3, not 50 : 40 : 10. Why?

  1. A finished job leaves the pool. Each job needs only 3 slices, so it can never run more than 3 times. When a big ticket holder finishes, its tickets leave with it. In the end the small job is alone and wins every draw. So more tickets didn't mean more CPU time in total. It meant running earlier and finishing sooner. (In lottery.c, that is the line total_tickets -= jobs[i].tickets.)
  2. A few draws are just noise. All three jobs competed for only 4 draws (draws 1–4). That is like tossing a coin 4 times. The counts can't settle to 50 : 40 : 10 that fast. Proportional share is a promise about long, continuous competition.
This is how it's asked You get a run log in which every process ran the same number of times despite very different tickets, and you're asked "shouldn't they run in proportion to their tickets? Explain." Answer in this order: (1) each job has a short, fixed burst, so it can only win that many times; (2) the high-ticket job tends to win early, finishes, and leaves the pool with its tickets; (3) the remaining jobs then split a smaller pool, and the last job wins every draw; (4) lottery only guarantees the ticket ratio over long-running competition. Go through the log and mark where each job leaves.

In short: lottery is fair only on average, over a long time. Measure it with U = first finish ÷ second finish (1 = fair). Short jobs break the ticket ratio because finished jobs leave the pool and a few draws are just luck.

Define the lottery unfairness metric U. Two equal jobs each need 50 time units; one finishes at 60 and the other at 100. What is U, and when does U get close to 1?

U = (time the first job completes) / (time the second job completes) = 60/100 = 0.6. U = 1 is perfectly fair. U gets close to 1 only when jobs run for a long time.

Lottery, tickets from 0: P has 80 (0–79), Q has 20 (80–99), and each job needs exactly 2 slices. The draws are 35, 91, 12. Who runs each time, and after that what are Q's range and chance of winning?

35 → P, 91 → Q, 12 → P. P has had its 2 slices and leaves with its 80 tickets. Now Q holds 0–19 out of 20, so Q wins 100% of the remaining draws. That is why short jobs don't follow their ticket ratios.

Two jobs with equal tickets each need 40 time units. Under lottery, one finishes at t = 48 and the other at t = 80. Which is TRUE?

  • U = 48 / 80 = 0.6. U = (time the first job completes) / (time the second job completes) = 48/80 = 0.6. Perfectly fair would be 1.
  • U = 80 / 48 ≈ 1.67. Upside down. The first completion goes on top, so U ≤ 1: 48/80 = 0.6.
  • U = 40 / 80 = 0.5. 40 is the run-time, not a completion time. U = 48/80 = 0.6.
  • U = 40 / 48 ≈ 0.83. Both numbers must be completion times: U = 48/80 = 0.6.

Which is TRUE about how fair lottery scheduling is?

  • U gets close to 1 only when the jobs run for a long time. OSTEP's study: with short jobs U is poor; over a long run the random draws average out.
  • U gets close to 1 only when the jobs are short. Reversed. Short jobs mean few draws, so luck dominates. Long jobs → U near 1.
  • Lottery hits the exact ticket ratio in every short window of time. That is stride. Lottery is right only on average (Dr. Bilal's slide: B wins 4 of 15 draws with 25% of the tickets).
  • U close to 0 means the two jobs finished at nearly the same time. Finishing together gives U close to 1. U close to 0 is very unfair.

Ticket mechanisms

The problem: tickets are a handy currency. What useful tricks can users and processes do with them?

The book lists three. Each one is like something you can do with real money.

MechanismWhat it doesWhen it's useful
Ticket currencyA user allocates tickets among their own jobs in whatever currency they like. The system converts that currency into the correct global value.Users manage their own jobs without worrying about everyone else's totals
Ticket transferA process temporarily hands off its tickets to another process.Client/server: a client waiting on a server lends it tickets so the work done on its behalf goes faster, and the server gives them back afterwards
Ticket inflationA process temporarily raises (or lowers) the number of tickets it owns.Only among processes that trust each other. A greedy process could otherwise give itself a huge number of tickets and take over the machine.

Bilal slides, Ch 9A p.7–8OSTEP §9.3

Currency, worked

Think of exchanging money. User X pays in "X-units", and the system converts them into real (global) tickets at X's exchange rate.

The system has 200 global tickets: user X owns 100 and user Y owns 100. Each user splits their own share in a private currency.

In short: currency = split your tickets in your own units (convert: local ÷ your total local × your global). Transfer = lend tickets to another process. Inflation = change your own ticket count, only safe among trusting processes.

Name the three ticket mechanisms and say what each does.

Ticket currency: a user splits their tickets among their own jobs in a local currency, converted to global tickets. Ticket transfer: a process temporarily hands its tickets to another (e.g. client → server). Ticket inflation: a process temporarily raises or lowers its own tickets; only safe among processes that trust each other.

A user owns 300 global tickets and issues 500 tickets of their own currency: 200 to job J1 and 300 to job J2. How many global tickets does each job hold?

global = (local / user's total local) × user's global.
J1 = 200/500 × 300 = 120. J2 = 300/500 × 300 = 180.

Which is TRUE about the ticket mechanisms?

  • Ticket transfer lets a client lend its tickets to a server that is working for it. The server runs faster on the client's behalf, then the tickets go back.
  • Ticket inflation lets a client lend its tickets to a server that is working for it. Lending tickets to another process is transfer. Inflation is a process raising or lowering its own ticket count.
  • Ticket currency lets a process raise its own ticket count when it needs more CPU. That is inflation. Currency lets a user split their tickets among their own jobs in local units, converted to global tickets.
  • Ticket inflation is safe even among processes that don't trust each other. Inflation only makes sense among processes that trust each other; a greedy process could give itself a huge number of tickets.

A user owns 60 global tickets and issues 400 tickets of their own currency: 100 to job J1 and 300 to job J2. Which is TRUE?

  • J1 holds 15 global tickets. global = local / user's total local × user's global = 100/400 × 60 = 15. (J2: 300/400 × 60 = 45.)
  • J1 holds 100 global tickets. 100 is J1's local count. Convert: 100/400 × 60 = 15.
  • J1 holds 25 global tickets. 25 is J1's percentage of the user's share (100/400 = 25%). Times 60 → 15.
  • J1 holds 45 global tickets. 45 is J2's value (300/400 × 60). J1 = 100/400 × 60 = 15.

Stride scheduling: lottery without the dice

The problem: lottery is only right on average, and short runs can be unlucky. Can we hit the exact shares every time, with no luck at all?

"Lottery scheduling is random; we can make it deterministic using stride scheduling." Deterministic means the same input always gives the same schedule.Bilal slides, Ch 9A p.14OSTEP §9.6

The three formulas stride = (a large number) / (number of tickets), for example 10,000 / tickets.
Every job starts with pass = 0.
Each time: run the job with the lowest pass, then pass += stride for that job only.

Two new terms. A job's stride is its step size: how much its counter grows each time it runs. Its pass is the counter itself: the total of all its steps so far.

Think of runners on a track. The scheduler always lets the runner who is furthest behind take the next step. A job with many tickets has a small stride, so it takes tiny steps. It stays behind, so it keeps getting another turn. A job with few tickets takes giant steps and quickly jumps ahead, so it waits longer.

Tiny example: big number 100. A has 50 tickets → stride 2. B has 25 tickets → stride 4. Each time B runs once, A runs twice. That matches 50 : 25.

curr = remove_min(queue);     // pick client with min pass
schedule(curr);               // run for quantum
curr->pass += curr->stride;   // update pass using stride
insert(queue, curr);          // return curr to queue

Stride scheduling with a large number of 10,000. A has 40 tickets, B has 160. Which is TRUE?

  • A's stride is 250 and B's is 62.5, so B's pass grows more slowly and B runs more often. stride = 10,000 / tickets. B's smaller stride means its pass climbs slowly, so it is the lowest more often: 4 runs for every 1 of A's (160 : 40).
  • A's stride is 250 and B's is 62.5, so A's pass grows more slowly and A runs more often. A's bigger stride makes its pass grow faster, so A runs less often.
  • A's stride is 40 and B's is 160, so B's pass grows faster and A runs more often. Tickets aren't the stride. Stride = 10,000 / tickets: A 250, B 62.5.
  • A's stride is 62.5 and B's is 250, so A's pass grows more slowly and A runs more often. Inverted. Stride = BIG / tickets, so more tickets give a smaller stride: A = 10,000/40 = 250.

Which is TRUE about stride scheduling?

  • After a job runs, its pass increases by its stride. pass += stride, so jobs with more tickets (smaller stride) climb more slowly and run more often.
  • After a job runs, its pass increases by its tickets. It increases by its stride (BIG / tickets), not its ticket count.
  • After a job runs, its stride increases by its pass. Swapped: pass grows by the stride; the stride never changes.
  • After a job runs, the job with the highest pass runs next. The lowest pass runs next.

Stride, large number 1,000. A has 10 tickets (pass 300), B has 5 (pass 250), C has 20 (pass 380). Which is TRUE about the next pick?

  • B runs (lowest pass) and its pass becomes 450. B's stride = 1,000/5 = 200, so 250 + 200 = 450. A and C don't change.
  • C runs (smallest stride) and its pass becomes 430. The pick is by lowest pass, not smallest stride. B (250) runs.
  • B runs (lowest pass) and its pass becomes 255. Add the stride (1,000/5 = 200), not the tickets: 250 + 200 = 450.
  • B runs (lowest pass), and then every job's pass grows by its own stride. Only the job that ran updates its pass: B → 450, while A stays 300 and C stays 380.

  • They are C A C A B C. That starts each pass at its stride. Every job starts at pass 0, so the first pick is a three-way tie → A.
  • They are A B C B A B. That adds tickets (A +4, B +2, C +6) instead of strides, which favours the job with fewer tickets. Pass grows by the stride.

  • jobs[i].pass <= jobs[min_pass_index].pass An equal pass does replace it, so the last tied job wins.
  • jobs[i].pass > jobs[min_pass_index].pass That finds the highest pass. Stride runs the lowest.
  • jobs[i].stride < jobs[min_pass_index].stride That always picks the job with the most tickets. The pick is by pass, not stride.

In stride.c (strides A 100, B 200, C 1000), the passes before a pick are A 300, B 400, C 1000. Which is TRUE about the next two picks?

  • A, then A: A moves to 400, and the 400–400 tie goes to A, listed first. 300 is lowest → A runs → 300 + 100 = 400. Then A and B tie, and the strict < keeps A.
  • A, then B: A moves to 400, and the 400–400 tie goes to B, which waited longer. stride.c has no "waited longer" rule. A tie goes to the first listed: A.
  • A, then B: A moves to 500, so B's 400 is now the lowest. A adds its own stride (100), not B's (200): 300 + 100 = 400, a tie that A wins.
  • C, then A: C has run the fewest times, so it goes first. The pick is by lowest pass, not by run count. C's 1000 is the highest.

Stride vs lottery

The problem: stride is exact, so why would anyone still use lottery?

Because stride has to remember a pass for every job. Lottery remembers almost nothing. That matters most when a new job arrives.

LotteryStride
FairnessProbabilistic: right on average, can be unfair over short runsDeterministic: exact proportions at the end of every cycle
State keptNo global state per process, just the ticket counts and the totalA pass value for every process (global state)

The new-job problem Say the others have passes around 1,000, and a new job joins with pass 0. It is now the lowest by a mile. So it wins every pick until it catches up. That's what Dr. Bilal's slide means by "If new job enters with pass value 0, it will monopolize the CPU!" A sensible fix is to start it at the current minimum pass. Linux's CFS does exactly this with min_vruntime (next lesson).Bilal slides, Ch 9A p.23

In short: stride is deterministic and exact but keeps a pass per job. Lottery is probabilistic but keeps no per-job state, so a new job is easy: just add its tickets. In stride, a newcomer at pass 0 monopolizes the CPU.

Stride scheduling: why is a new job that starts with pass 0 a problem, and what is a sensible fix?

Everyone else's pass is already large, so the newcomer has the lowest pass by far and monopolises the CPU until it catches up. Fix: start it at the current minimum pass (CFS does the same with min_vruntime).

Lottery vs stride: which one is deterministic, which keeps per-process global state, and which handles a new job easily?

Stride is deterministic (exact proportions every cycle) but keeps a pass for every process. Lottery is probabilistic, keeps no global state beyond tickets and the total, and handles a new job easily (just add its tickets).

Which is TRUE about adding a new job part-way through?

  • Lottery just adds its tickets; in stride, a newcomer at pass 0 would monopolize the CPU. Everyone else's pass is already large, so a pass-0 newcomer is the lowest for a long time and wins every pick until it catches up.
  • Stride just starts it at pass 0; in lottery, a newcomer would monopolize the CPU. Swapped. Lottery keeps no per-job history, so a newcomer just adds tickets. It is stride where pass 0 hogs the CPU.
  • Lottery just adds its tickets; in stride, a newcomer at pass 0 would starve until it caught up. Reversed. The lowest pass runs, so a pass-0 newcomer runs every time (monopolizes) until it catches up.
  • Lottery just adds its tickets; in stride, every other job's stride must be recomputed. A stride is BIG / that job's own tickets; it doesn't depend on the total, so nobody else's stride changes. The problem is the newcomer's pass.

Which is TRUE when comparing lottery and stride scheduling?

  • Stride is deterministic and exact every cycle; lottery is right only on average over time. Stride keeps a pass per job and always picks the lowest, so proportions come out exactly. Lottery draws random winners.
  • Lottery is deterministic and exact every cycle; stride is right only on average over time. Swapped. Lottery draws random tickets; stride is the deterministic one.
  • Both are deterministic: once tickets are set, the same schedule repeats every run. Lottery is probabilistic: each slice's winner is a random draw.
  • Stride is deterministic and, like lottery, keeps no per-job global state. Stride keeps a pass for every job (global state). Lottery keeps only the tickets and the total.

Practice

Answer from memory first, then check. Struggling a little is what makes it stick.

1. Tickets: Red 4, Green 7, Blue 9, numbered from 0 in that order. The winning ticket is 10. Who runs?

Total 20. Red 0–3, Green 4–10, Blue 11–19. Ticket 10 is the last one in Green's range, so Green runs. (Numbered from 1 instead, the ranges would be 1–4, 5–11, 12–20, and 10 would still be Green.)

2. The list is C (30) → A (50) → B (20). The draw is 60, and the code stops when counter > winner. Which job runs?

C: counter 30, and 30 > 60 is false. A: counter 80, and 80 > 60 is true, so stop. A runs.

3. Stride scheduling with a large number of 10,000. P has 25 tickets, Q has 100 and R has 50. All passes start at 0 and ties go to the job listed first (P, Q, R). In the first 7 picks, how many times does Q run?

Strides: P 400, Q 100, R 200. Rows: (0,0,0) P → (400,0,0) Q → (400,100,0) R → (400,100,200) Q → (400,200,200) Q → (400,300,200) R → (400,300,400) Q. Q runs 4 times, P once and R twice, which is the 25 : 100 : 50 = 1 : 4 : 2 ratio exactly.

4. Large number 10,000. J1 has 60 tickets (pass 700), J2 has 40 tickets (pass 900), J3 has 20 tickets (pass 1,000). The scheduler picks a job. What is that job's pass after it runs?

Lowest pass is J1 (700), so J1 runs. Its stride is 10,000 / 60 = 166.67. New pass = 700 + 166.67 = 866.67 (866 if you rounded the stride down to 166).

5. Two jobs, each with the same tickets and a runtime of 30, run under lottery. One finishes at time 30 and the other at time 48. What is the unfairness metric U?

U = first completion / second completion = 30 / 48 = 0.625. Perfectly fair would be close to 1.

6. A user owns 200 global tickets and issues 1,000 tickets of their own currency. One of their jobs gets 250 of those. How many global tickets is that?

250 / 1,000 = 1/4 of the user's share, and 1/4 × 200 = 50 global tickets.

7. Why is adding a new job harder for stride scheduling than for lottery scheduling?

Stride keeps a pass for every job (global state). A newcomer starting at pass 0 is far below everyone else and wins every pick until it catches up. Lottery has no per-job history: it just adds the new tickets to the total.
8. A lottery log shows jobs with 70, 20 and 10 tickets, each with a 4-slice burst. Each one ran exactly 4 times out of 12. Explain in 3–4 sentences why this doesn't break proportional share.
Each job only needs 4 slices, so none of them can ever run more than 4 times. The 70-ticket job wins most of the early draws, finishes first, and leaves the pool with its tickets. The 20-ticket job then dominates the smaller pool until it finishes too. Finally the 10-ticket job holds 100% of the remaining tickets and wins every draw. Tickets decided who ran earlier (and finished sooner), not the total count. Lottery only promises ticket-proportional CPU time when jobs compete for a long time.
